# dorylus_prey
####Abstract#### 
Driver ants of the genus Dorylus are among the most dominant predators in 
Afrotropical ecosystems and have been proposed as keystone species due to their
massive swarm raids. Despite their suggested influences on their ecosystem,
quantitative data on the dietary composition and worker biometrics remain scarce. 
To address this gap, I investigated the worker biometrics and allometric
relationships within Dorylus (Anomma) sjostedti and Dorylus (Anomma) wilverthi
based on 2933 samples collected across 25 raid events in lowland primary and
secondary rainforest in mainland Equatorial Guinea. Further I identified prey 
foraged by the driver ants to order level and measured prey item metrics to gain
insight into the dietary composition and allometric rules of shared labour
within the prey transport.
I found that both species exhibited continuous worker polymorphism but differed 
in demographic structure. D. wilverthi showed three distinct worker size modes, 
whereas D. sjostedti exhibited only two. Despite this difference, both species 
followed the same allometric scaling relationship between head width and body
length, as well as between body mass and carried prey mass, indicating shared 
biomechanical constraints. Prey weight increased strongly with worker body
weight, while load relative to worker weight decreased, demonstrating size 
dependent division of labor. Cooperative transport was primarily associated 
with the retrieval of large prey items rather than with reducing individual 
load. Dietary analyses revealed an almost complete overlap in prey spectra 
between the two species, with no significant differences in prey composition or 
prey life-stage usage. Observed differences in prey composition emerged between 
forest types, highlighting forest type as a driver of variation in prey 
availability, though even these significant differences in diet were quite 
small. Overall, I could show that D. wilverthi and D. sjostedti occupy a nearly 
identical trophic niche at the order level and are best characterized as 
opportunistic generalist predators.
